Two missions. Four explorers.

Each Viking mission carried an orbiter and a lander. They traveled together, then separated at Mars.

One partner stays above; one travels down.

Orbiter: the view from above

Solar panels powered cameras and instruments that studied Mars from orbit.

Lander: the laboratory below

Three legs supported a stationary laboratory. Two RTGs made electricity from radioactive heat.

Did Viking find life?

The soil produced puzzling chemical activity. Tap each step to examine the evidence.

1 · Observation
The biology experiments measured how samples responded under different test conditions. Some responses looked intriguing.
2 · Possible explanation
Living microbes could be one explanation for certain signals. Nonliving soil chemistry could also produce reactions.
3 · What can we conclude?
Taken together, the experiments did not provide clear evidence of living microorganisms at either landing site. A surprising signal needs supporting evidence.
A GOOD SCIENCE QUESTION
“What else could explain this?” Asking that question helps scientists test an idea.

Mars came into focus

Surface photography
The landers returned thousands of photographs, showing rocks, soil, and the view from two places on Mars.
Weather watching
Sensors tracked temperature, wind, and pressure. Repeated readings showed how the Martian environment changed over time.
Mapping from orbit
Together, the orbiters returned more than 52,000 images and mapped about 97% of Mars at roughly 300 meters per picture element.

The search keeps going

Each spacecraft had its own working life. The landers stayed on Mars while their orbiters watched from above.
