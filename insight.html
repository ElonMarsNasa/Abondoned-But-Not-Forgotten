A quiet place for a big question

InSight landed at Elysium Planitia on November 26, 2018. A flat, safe place was ideal for setting instruments on the ground.

Elysium Planitia: a landing site near the Martian equator.

An instrument on the ground

InSight’s arm placed a seismometer on the surface. A protective shield helped reduce interference from wind and temperature changes.

Prepare to place the seismometer beside the lander.

Mars is shaking

InSight detected marsquakes from breaking rocks and impacts.

A quiet trace becomes a pattern of vibrations. This is an illustrative signal.

Seeing with vibrations

Seismic waves change as they travel through different materials. Their patterns help scientists investigate the planet’s interior.

Crust: the outer rocky layer of Mars.

The mole meets a challenge

A heat probe nicknamed the “mole” was meant to burrow into the ground. The soil did not provide the friction it needed to reach its intended depth.

With enough friction, soil can resist the hammer’s recoil and help a probe move down.

Dust dims the sunlight

Dust accumulated on InSight’s solar panels, reducing its energy. NASA retired the mission in December 2022.

Clean panels allow more sunlight to reach the solar cells.
